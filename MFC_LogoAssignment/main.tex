\section*{Concept}
\textbf{Zenith Orbit} is an imaginary technology company. The logo is a radiating
sun/orbit emblem built from two concentric circles, eight symmetric rays, a curved
base arc, a small crown accent, and a wavy underline. The design is built around a
single \textbf{vertical mirror axis} ($x=0$).

Inequalities (\#3, \#4) shade the core and ring band with color. Symmetry is
clearly visible: rays \#5/\#8 and \#6/\#7 are mirror pairs across the vertical axis,

symmetric about the $y$-axis since they contain only $x^2$ terms.

\section*{Mathematics Behind the Design}
The logo is anchored by two concentric circles, $x^2+y^2=20.25$ and
$x^2+y^2=5.35$, which are inherently symmetric under any rotation since they depend
only on $x^2+y^2$; the inequalities \#3 and \#4 shade the core and the ring band
between them without needing any extra curves. The eight rays come from just four
lines -- $y=x$, $y=-x$, $x=0$, and $y=0$ -- each restricted to a short interval on
both sides of the origin, so domain restrictions turn four infinite lines into
eight finite, evenly spaced spokes. The crown was built from two ordinary linear
pieces, $y=x+7.1$ and $y=-x+7.1$, placed on mirrored domains so they meet at a
single peak and form a V-shape without using an absolute value function. The
quadratic base arc, $y=-0.09x^2-4.35$, curves gently because its coefficient is
small and negative, and being a pure $x^2$ expression it is automatically
symmetric. The sine wave underline adds a repeating, flowing accent that none of
the other function types could produce on their own.

\section*{Explanation}
This logo uses four different types of mathematical functions: linear, quadratic,
circular, and trigonometric functions. Circle equations formed the outer ring and
core, linear equations built all eight rays, the two-sided crown, and were
restricted with domains to keep them as short segments instead of infinite lines,
the quadratic function created the curved base arc, and a sine function produced
the wavy underline. Inequalities were used on the two circle equations to shade
the core and the ring band with distinct colors. The main challenge was getting
the domain restrictions on each ray to line up exactly with the circle radii, so
the rays appeared to start right at the edge of the ring instead of overlapping it
or leaving a gap. I also had to adjust the crown's two linear pieces carefully so
they met at a single point. During this assignment, I learned how restricting the
domain of ordinary lines can produce complex radial symmetry, and how inequalities
can shade regions between two curves.
